Growth Elasticities, Distributional Shifts, and the Post-2015 Deceleration in Global Extreme Poverty Reduction.

An analysis of World Bank poverty data (1990-2025) examining regional trends and the roles of income growth and inequality.

In January 2026, the base subway fare in New York went up to $3. That is also the World Bank's international poverty line. The line is adjusted for differences in prices across countries, so falling below it means living on less than what $3 buys in the United States. By the World Bank's most recent estimate, about 847 million people were below this line in 2024.

The decline in extreme poverty between 1990 and 2015 was fast enough that the United Nations adopted the goal of ending it by 2030. Based on the data that goal is out of reach today. I wanted to understand why progress slowed, and whether the slowdown reflects a global trend or developments in particular regions. Using the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform, I examined poverty estimates for 195 countries and regions from 1990 to 2025.

Most of the decline before 2015 took place in Asia. In 1990, more than half of the world's extremely poor people lived in East Asia, and over the following twenty-five years the region's poverty rate fell to a few percent. Decomposing the global trend by region, I find that East Asia accounts for roughly two-thirds of the fall in the world poverty rate between 1990 and 2015, and South Asia for most of the rest. Both regions have continued to reduce poverty since then, but East Asia now has comparatively little extreme poverty left to reduce. Part of the global slowdown is therefore a consequence of earlier success.

Sub-Saharan Africa followed a different path. Its poverty rate declined, but much more slowly, while its population grew rapidly. As a result, the number of people in extreme poverty in the region rose from about 320 million in 1990 to nearly 600 million in 2025, and the region now accounts for roughly seven in ten of the world's extreme poor. According to the World Bank, the region's poverty rate only returned to its pre-pandemic level in 2025. Sub-Saharan Africa is not the only region where progress has stalled: the World Bank report,the World Bank reports that extreme poverty has recently risen in the Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan and Pakistan region. But it is now where the global trend is largely determined.

To understand what changed within African countries, I compared household surveys conducted in the same country at different points in time, using only pairs the World Bank classifies as comparable. For each pair, I decomposed the change in poverty into a growth component, reflecting changes in average income, and a distribution component, reflecting changes in how income is shared. Before 2015, growth was the main source of poverty reduction in Africa, as it was elsewhere. After 2015, this pattern reversed. Average income declined in more than half of the African survey comparisons, so that growth on balance increased poverty. Income became somewhat more equally distributed over the same period, which largely offset this increase, leaving poverty broadly unchanged. In the rest of the world, the contribution of growth weakened after 2015 but remained positive.

A second pattern compounds the first. Across countries, the same rate of income growth is associated with a much smaller reduction in poverty in Sub-Saharan Africa, about a third of the reduction observed elsewhere. The association is weaker where inequality and initial poverty are higher, both of which characterize much of the region. This suggests that a recovery in growth would not, by itself, reduce African poverty at the pace previously seen in Asia.

These findings carry more weight in the current context of declining aid. Oxfam estimates that G7 aid spending in 2026 will be more than a quarter below its 2024 level. This analysis does not measure the effects of those cuts, but it does indicate that they coincide with a period in which poverty reduction in the poorest countries had already stalled. Estimates for the most recent years are World Bank projections rather than survey results and are likely to be revised as new data become available.
