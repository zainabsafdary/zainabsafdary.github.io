Labor Market Integration of Urban Refugees: Evidence on Employment Outcomes and Barriers to Formal Work in Kampala, Uganda.

Comparing refugees and Ugandan nationals in Kampala's labor market

In 2021, photos of Sayed Ahmad Shah Sadat ,Afghanistan's former Minister of Communications, delivering food on a bicycle in Leipzig spread around the world. With two master's degrees from Oxford and more than twenty years of experience in telecommunications, his story became a symbol of brain waste : skilled migrants working in jobs far below their training. I wanted to know whether this picture holds for refugees more broadly. Using the World Bank's 2022 Host and Refugee Labor Market Survey , I compared 1,028 refugees and 876 Ugandan nationals living in the same Kampala neighborhoods. Uganda is one of the few countries that gives refugees the legal right to work , which makes it a good test of whether legal access is enough. The answer surprised me. The bigger problem is not that refugees work below their skills. It's that most of them don't work at all.

Only 29% of refugee adults in Kampala are employed, compared with 68% of Ugandan nationals. Among people actively looking for work, about half of refugees are unemployed, compared with 15% of nationals. This gap of roughly 40 percentage points barely changes after accounting for age, sex, education, and English ability.

What about the refugees who do find work? If brain waste were the main story, we would expect them to report jobs that fit their skills poorly.

Refugees who do find work are more likely than nationals to say their job closely matches their skills (65% compared with 46%). This is likely a selection effect. When fewer than a third of refugees are working, the ones who are employed are probably those whose skills already fit Kampala's economy. Sadat's story is one of mismatch, but in Kampala, the more common story is a refugee with no job at all.

Employment rises from 11% among refugees who arrived in the last three years to about 45% among those who have been in Uganda for more than five years. This suggests the first years after arrival are when support matters most, though part of the pattern may reflect differences between arrival groups rather than time spent in Uganda.
